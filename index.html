Swiggy Smart Reorder

Repeat customers order the same few dishes, yet they still browse for minutes at dinner peak. Let the app remember for them.

Problem

Dinner hours carry the most orders and the slowest deliveries. Long browsing at checkout and late kitchen signals add minutes to every order.

Users

Repeat customers ordering three or more times a month, and restaurant partners who need predictable peak demand.

AI solution

A one tap reorder card ranked by a model using order history, time of day and kitchen prep time, plus an early demand signal to the restaurant when a reorder is likely.

Success metrics

Time to checkout, reorder conversion, peak hour delivery time, cancellation rate.

MVP

Top three reorder suggestions for repeat users in one city, A/B tested against the current home screen for four weeks.

Risks & guardrails

Stale menus, repetitive suggestions and privacy expectations. Show why each suggestion appears and keep an easy opt out.

Self-initiated product concept Quick commerce · AI forecasting

Blinkit Stock Sense

Ten minute delivery breaks the moment a dark store runs out of milk at 8 am. Predict it two hours earlier.

Problem

Fast moving items stock out at predictable peaks, so orders get split, delayed or cancelled exactly when demand is highest.

Users

Dark store managers who plan replenishment, and customers who expect what they want to be in stock.

AI solution

Hour level demand forecasts per category for each store, feeding a restock alert two hours before a predicted stockout.

Success metrics

Fill rate, stockout risk hours, share of orders delivered under 10 minutes, forecast accuracy.

MVP

Forecast the top 200 items for three stores, push alerts to the store manager app, and compare against manual planning.

Risks & guardrails

Weather and festival spikes, and new items with no history. Fall back to simple rules when the model is unsure.

Self-initiated product concept Fintech · AI reliability

Paytm Smart Retry

A failed UPI payment at a shop counter costs more than one sale. It costs trust in the app and in the merchant.

Problem

Many UPI failures are not the user's fault. Bank server downtime and timeouts fail payments at busy hours, and the user is left guessing whether to pay again.

Users

Everyday UPI users paying at shops, and small merchants who lose sales and time when a payment fails at the counter.

AI solution

A model that scores the health of each bank route in real time, warns the user before paying through a struggling bank, and offers a one tap retry through another linked account or UPI Lite.

Success metrics

Payment success rate, failed payments recovered, time to pay, and repeat failures per user.

MVP

Merchant QR payments in two cities: a bank health warning plus a one tap retry, measured against a control group for four weeks.

Risks & guardrails

Wrong warnings and double payments. The retry only happens when the user taps, the status of the first payment is always shown, and the flow follows NPCI rules.

Practo No-Show Shield

An empty slot in a doctor's day is care one patient didn't get and income the clinic didn't earn.

Problem

Patients book OPD appointments and don't turn up, while others wait days for a slot. Clinics find out only when the chair stays empty.

Users

Patients booking appointments, clinic front desks managing the day's schedule, and doctors who want full, predictable days.

AI solution

A no-show risk score for every booking. High risk patients get a WhatsApp reminder with one tap rescheduling, and freed slots go to the waitlist automatically.

Success metrics

No-show rate, slots refilled, days a patient waits for the next slot, and doctor utilisation.

MVP

Fifty clinics in one city across three specialities, with reminders and waitlist refills, measured against control clinics.

Risks & guardrails

The score never changes who gets care, no medical records go into the model, patients can opt out of reminders, and data handling follows India's DPDP Act.

Self-initiated product concept Supply chain · AI risk scoring

Delhivery RTO Shield

Every parcel that comes back is paid for twice: once on the way out, once on the way home.

Problem

Cash on delivery orders that are refused or undeliverable return to origin. RTO burns two way shipping, ties up stock for weeks and eats the margin of small D2C sellers.

Users

D2C sellers shipping through Delhivery, and last mile teams who make the failed delivery attempts.

AI solution

An RTO risk score on every order at the moment it is placed. Risky COD orders get an address check, a prepaid nudge or a WhatsApp delivery slot confirmation before they ship.

Success metrics

RTO rate, cost per delivered order, first attempt delivery rate, and orders lost to the extra step.

MVP

COD orders for two seller categories, risk based checks against no checks, for six weeks.

Risks & guardrails

Genuine buyers in smaller towns must not be punished. Checks stay light, the seller sees why an order was flagged, and COD is never switched off.

Analytics Casebook

Break it down

One marketing analytics problem, solved the way I would solve it at work: frame it, split it into pieces, size each piece, find the leak, then fix it and prove it.

Marketing analytics · Practice case

D2C skincare ROAS drop

A D2C skincare brand spends ₹10 lakh a month on Meta ads. Over one quarter, revenue from those ads fell from ₹38 lakh to ₹26 lakh while spend stayed flat. The founder's question is simple: what broke, and what do we fix first?

Sample numbers, built to practise the full method end to end.

Monthly ad spend₹10L flat
Revenue from ads₹38L → ₹26L
Return on ad spend3.8 → 2.6 −32%
01Frame it

Turn the complaint into a formula

"Ads aren't working" is a feeling, not a problem.

ROAS is revenue divided by ad spend, and it splits cleanly into four levers I can measure one at a time.

ROAS = CTR × Conversion rate × Order value ÷ Cost per 1,000 impressions × 1,000
02Break it down

Put a number on every lever

Same campaigns, start of the quarter against the end of it. Three levers got worse. One got a lot worse.

ROAS3.8 → 2.6−32%
Click through rate1.6% → 1.5%−6%
Conversion rate3.0% → 2.4%−20%
Order value₹1,425 → ₹1,400−2%
Cost per 1,000 views₹180 → ₹195+8%, worse
03Size it

Which lever cost the most?

Splitting the 1.2 point fall across the four levers shows conversion rate alone explains about 0.7 of it. Costlier impressions and a tired creative explain most of the rest. So conversion is where I dig first.

04Find the leak

Split conversion until the cause shows up

By device, desktop conversion holds steady near 3.6%. Mobile falls from 2.8% to 1.9% in week 7, the same week a new landing page went live. Mobile load time had gone from 2.1 to 4.6 seconds. Seven in ten visitors are on mobile, so that one page change drags the whole account down.

MobileDesktop
05Fix and prove

Fix the biggest leak first, then prove it

Fix 1

Speed up mobile

Test the old, faster landing page against the new one on half of mobile traffic, and compress the heavy hero images.

ROAS back to about 3.1
Fix 2

Refresh creatives

Frequency had climbed to 4.1, so people kept seeing the same ad and clicked less. Rotate three new creatives.

About 3.3 with both fixes
Fix 3

Watch it daily

A small dashboard with mobile conversion, page load time, frequency and CPM, and an alert when mobile conversion drops below 2.4%.

Catch the next leak in days

Operations casebook

Find the bottleneck

An operations problem solved the way I'd solve it on the floor: map the flow, time every step, find the constraint, fix it, then lock the gain in.

Operations · Practice case

Dark store turnaround

A quick commerce dark store promises 10 minute delivery, but at the evening peak it takes 13 minutes just to get an order out of the door, before the rider even starts. Riders wait, orders stack up and late deliveries climb.

Sample timings, built to practise the full method end to end.

Peak orders per hour60
In-store time per order13.0 → 7.8 min −40%
Orders waiting at peak13 → 8 Little's Law
01Map it

Map the flow and time every step

Four steps sit between "order placed" and "rider leaves": waiting for a picker, picking, packing, and handing over to the rider. I time 50 orders at the peak hour. Picking is the biggest block, and nearly half the time is waiting, not working.

Before
Wait 2.5Pick 5.5Pack 2.0Handover 3.0
13.0 min
After
1.0Pick 3.5Pack 1.81.5
7.8 min
02Find it

Ask why until the cause shows up

Why is picking slow? Pickers walk the whole store. Why? The 50 fastest selling items are spread across every aisle. Why? Shelves were laid out by category, not by how often items sell. Little's Law confirms the pain: 60 orders an hour at 13 minutes each means about 13 orders are always in progress for 4 pickers.

03Fix it

Fix the constraint, not everything

Fix 1

Re-slot fast movers

ABC analysis on two weeks of orders. The top 50 items move to a golden zone next to the packing table.

Pick 5.5 → 3.5 min
Fix 2

Batch at peak

From 6 to 10 pm, pickers collect two or three orders per trip from the same zone instead of one.

Wait 2.5 → 1.0 min
Fix 3

Rider handover bay

Packed orders go to numbered slots, and riders scan and leave without waiting for staff.

Handover 3.0 → 1.5 min
04Lock it in

Make the gain stick

A one page SOP for each new step, a daily dashboard of pick time per order and the share of orders out in under 8 minutes, a weekly re-slotting check as demand shifts, and a control chart so a slide back is caught in days, not months.
